# Part 6.6 — Measure Replication Lag Correctly

**Target:** PostgreSQL 18
**Status:** Revised Final / Canonical Edition — Pending Repository Validation
**Lab:** `labs/replication-lag-sampler.sql`
**Classification:** SAFE-READ

## Purpose
Part 6.5 proved WAL transport and replay. Part 6.6 measures how far replication stages are separated and how those measurements change across an observation window.

```text
primary WAL -> sent -> written -> flushed -> replayed
```

Replication lag is not one universal number.

## Scope
Covers byte-based WAL distance, sender-stage distances, standby receive-to-replay backlog, PostgreSQL acknowledgment-delay fields, replay timestamp caveats, repeated observations, workload context, policy thresholds, and PASS/FAIL/UNKNOWN/CONTRADICTORY interpretation.

Synchronous policy belongs to 6.7, slot/WAL retention to 6.8, hot-standby conflicts to 6.9, and failover to 6.12+.

## Explicit WAL Distances
`pg_wal_lsn_diff(lsn1, lsn2)` returns the byte difference `lsn1 - lsn2`. Argument order matters.

Use explicit names:
- `current_to_sent_bytes`
- `sent_to_write_bytes`
- `sent_to_flush_bytes`
- `sent_to_replay_bytes`
- `current_to_replay_bytes`
- `receive_to_replay_bytes`

Do not hide unexpected negative results with `ABS()`. Unexpected ordering is contradictory evidence requiring investigation.

## Primary-Side Measurements
For each directly connected replication client, `pg_stat_replication` exposes sender-stage LSNs and `write_lag`, `flush_lag`, and `replay_lag`.

A byte distance is not automatically RPO, predicted data loss, catch-up time, or application impact. PostgreSQL lag fields are acknowledgment-delay evidence, not universal catch-up timers.

## Standby Receive-to-Replay Backlog
On a standby, compare `pg_last_wal_receive_lsn()` with `pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()` using `pg_wal_lsn_diff()`.

If receive-to-replay backlog grows across samples while receive LSN advances, local replay backlog is growing. That observation alone does not prove root cause.

## Replay Timestamp Trap
`pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()` identifies the timestamp associated with the last transaction replayed during recovery.

Do not equate `clock_timestamp() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()` with universal replication lag. An idle primary may have no new transactions while the standby is caught up.

## NULL Is Not Zero
Do not use `COALESCE(metric, 0)` for safety-critical replication metrics. Missing, unavailable, not-applicable, or privilege-limited evidence must remain distinguishable from zero.

Required evidence that cannot be established is UNKNOWN.

## One Sample Is Not a Trend
A point-in-time backlog does not show whether a standby is catching up, stable, or falling farther behind.

```text
900 MB -> 700 MB -> 400 MB -> 100 MB   backlog decreasing
900 MB -> 1.1 GB -> 1.5 GB -> 2.0 GB   backlog increasing
```

Do not assign root cause from trend alone.

## Sampling Model
The lab collects one fresh timestamped sample per invocation. For a short troubleshooting window, invoke it repeatedly from the client:

```bash
for i in {1..6}; do
    psql "$PGURI" -v sample_id="$i" -f labs/replication-lag-sampler.sql
    sleep 10
done
```

Use approved libpq authentication. Do not embed passwords or secrets. The interval is illustrative, not universal policy.

The SQL does not implement a server-side sleep loop or long-running transaction.

## Observation Time
Every sample records `clock_timestamp()`. If rates are calculated externally, use actual elapsed time rather than assuming the configured sleep interval occurred exactly.

## Workload and Maintenance Context
Interpret distance and trend alongside WAL generation, replay capacity, workload phase, and known maintenance such as standby restart, network/storage work, base backup activity, batch processing, bulk ingestion, or index maintenance.

Do not present backlog divided by recent rate as a guaranteed catch-up ETA.

## Thresholds
The lab does not invent universal warning or critical values.

Thresholds come from approved service policy, including RPO, read-staleness tolerance, HA design, workload, WAL generation profile, capacity, maintenance policy, and SLO.

```text
measurement -> trend -> service policy -> decision
```

No approved threshold means threshold compliance is UNKNOWN.

## Decision Model
**PASS:** required metrics are available and the observation window complies with approved service policy.

**FAIL:** approved lag policy is breached for its defined duration/window.

**UNKNOWN:** evidence is missing, stale, inaccessible, incomparable, or required policy is undefined.

**CONTRADICTORY:** measurements violate expected ordering/topology or disagree. Investigate; never convert to PASS.

## Stop Conditions
Stop ordinary lag analysis for an unexpected writable standby, unexpected role transition, suspected multiple writers, unexpected timeline/topology, or wrong target. Move to the appropriate fencing/failover/incident procedure.

## Key Takeaways
- Replication lag is multi-stage.
- Byte distance, acknowledgment delay, replay timestamp age, RPO, and catch-up ETA are different concepts.
- Receive-to-replay bytes expose local replay backlog.
- Trend is usually more useful than a single snapshot.
- NULL must not become zero.
- Unexpected negative distances must not be hidden.
- Thresholds belong to service policy.
- Measurement and remediation remain separate.

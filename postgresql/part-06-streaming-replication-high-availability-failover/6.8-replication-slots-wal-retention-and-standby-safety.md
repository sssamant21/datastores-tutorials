# 6.8 — Replication Slots, WAL Retention, and Standby Safety

## Purpose

Physical replication slots help protect a standby or other replication consumer from losing WAL that it still needs.

That protection creates an operational responsibility:

> PostgreSQL may retain WAL for a replication slot even when the consumer is slow, disconnected, or abandoned.

A production DBA or SRE must therefore understand both sides of the design:

```text
Replication slot
      |
      +--> protects required WAL
      |
      +--> can increase WAL retention
                    |
                    v
               pg_wal growth
                    |
                    v
             filesystem pressure
                    |
                    v
           PostgreSQL availability risk
```

This tutorial builds a production-focused operating model for PostgreSQL 18 replication slots, WAL retention, standby safety, monitoring, troubleshooting, and controlled remediation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

- explain why physical replication slots are used;
- explain how a slot can retain WAL for a disconnected consumer;
- interpret `active`, `active_pid`, `restart_lsn`, `wal_status`, and `safe_wal_size`;
- use PostgreSQL 18 evidence such as `inactive_since` and `invalidation_reason`;
- distinguish `wal_keep_size` from `max_slot_wal_keep_size`;
- understand the role of `idle_replication_slot_timeout`;
- estimate WAL distance from a slot's `restart_lsn`;
- distinguish WAL-distance evidence from actual filesystem consumption;
- identify inactive, abandoned, invalidated, and lost-slot risk;
- investigate `pg_wal` growth safely;
- define monitoring and capacity requirements; and
- follow a controlled decision process before removing a replication slot.

---

## 1. Why Replication Slots Exist

A physical standby normally receives WAL from the primary.

Conceptually:

```text
Primary
   |
   +---------------- WAL ----------------> Standby
```

If the standby disconnects, PostgreSQL can continue generating WAL.

Without sufficient WAL retention, WAL required by the disconnected standby can eventually become unavailable. The standby might then be unable to resume normally and may require recovery from another source or reconstruction.

A physical replication slot records WAL retention requirements for its consumer.

Conceptually:

```text
Primary
   |
   +--> Physical replication slot
   |          |
   |          +--> restart_lsn
   |                    |
   |                    v
   |             required WAL retained
   |
   +-------------------------------> Standby
```

This makes replication slots valuable for standby safety.

But the protection is not free.

---

## 2. The Production Risk

Consider a standby that stops consuming WAL:

```text
Standby unavailable
        |
        v
slot becomes inactive
        |
        v
restart_lsn stops advancing
        |
        v
primary continues generating WAL
        |
        v
required WAL accumulates
        |
        v
pg_wal storage pressure
```

If retention is not appropriately bounded and monitored, an abandoned or stalled slot can contribute to severe disk pressure.

The correct production lesson is therefore:

```text
Slot protection
      +
Retention controls
      +
Capacity monitoring
      +
Consumer monitoring
      +
Lifecycle management
```

Do not deploy replication slots without an operating model for all five.

---

## 3. Inspecting Replication Slots

Start with:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    active,
    active_pid,
    restart_lsn,
    wal_status,
    safe_wal_size
FROM pg_replication_slots
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

PostgreSQL 18 exposes additional useful lifecycle evidence, including:

```text
inactive_since
invalidation_reason
```

A more complete inspection is:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    active,
    active_pid,
    inactive_since,
    restart_lsn,
    wal_status,
    safe_wal_size,
    invalidation_reason
FROM pg_replication_slots
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

Always interpret these values together.

---

## 4. `active` and `active_pid`

The `active` column indicates whether a slot is currently being used.

Typical observations:

```text
active = true
```

means the slot currently has an active consumer.

```text
active = false
```

means it does not currently have an active consumer.

Do not automatically classify:

```text
active = false
```

as failure.

Possible explanations include:

- standby restart;
- maintenance;
- temporary network interruption;
- planned consumer shutdown;
- failed consumer;
- abandoned slot.

The difference can only be established with operational context.

`active_pid` identifies the process using an active slot when applicable.

---

## 5. `restart_lsn`

For a replication slot, `restart_lsn` represents the oldest WAL location that might still be required by the slot's consumer.

Operationally:

```text
current WAL position
        |
        | WAL generated since restart_lsn
        |
restart_lsn
```

If a consumer stops progressing, `restart_lsn` may stop advancing while the primary continues generating WAL.

That makes `restart_lsn` one of the most important slot-capacity signals.

---

## 6. Estimating WAL Distance

On a primary, a useful evidence calculation is:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    restart_lsn,
    pg_wal_lsn_diff(
        pg_current_wal_lsn(),
        restart_lsn
    ) AS wal_distance_bytes
FROM pg_replication_slots
WHERE restart_lsn IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

For readability:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    restart_lsn,
    pg_size_pretty(
        pg_wal_lsn_diff(
            pg_current_wal_lsn(),
            restart_lsn
        )
    ) AS wal_distance
FROM pg_replication_slots
WHERE restart_lsn IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

### Important limitation

This is WAL-position distance.

It is **not** an exact measurement of physical disk space attributable solely to the slot.

Actual `pg_wal` usage can also be affected by:

- checkpoints;
- WAL recycling;
- archiving;
- other replication slots;
- `wal_keep_size`;
- other WAL retention requirements; and
- current workload behavior.

Use WAL distance as evidence, not as filesystem accounting.

---

## 7. Understanding `wal_status`

PostgreSQL reports slot WAL availability through `wal_status`.

Important states include:

```text
reserved
extended
unreserved
lost
```

### `reserved`

Required WAL is currently retained within the normal retention behavior relevant to the slot.

### `extended`

Required WAL is being retained beyond ordinary WAL reservation because of slot requirements or related retention behavior.

### `unreserved`

The slot no longer has all of its required WAL safely reserved under the configured retention limit.

Required WAL can be removed at a subsequent checkpoint.

This condition requires investigation.

Do not automatically treat it as identical to `lost`.

### `lost`

Required WAL has been removed and the slot is no longer usable for its intended continuation.

Operational classification:

```text
wal_status = lost
        |
        v
CRITICAL
```

The consumer may require recovery or reconstruction depending on its type and architecture.

---

## 8. Understanding `safe_wal_size`

`safe_wal_size` helps indicate how much additional WAL can be generated before a slot is in danger of becoming lost under the configured slot-retention limit.

Inspect:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    wal_status,
    safe_wal_size
FROM pg_replication_slots
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

Do not implement:

```text
safe_wal_size IS NULL
        =
FAIL
```

A `NULL` value requires context.

For example, it can occur when the slot is already lost or when slot WAL retention is unlimited because:

```text
max_slot_wal_keep_size = -1
```

Interpret:

```text
safe_wal_size
+
wal_status
+
max_slot_wal_keep_size
```

together.

---

## 9. `invalidation_reason`

PostgreSQL 18 exposes slot invalidation evidence through:

```text
invalidation_reason
```

Depending on slot type and condition, this can identify why a slot became invalid.

Important operational examples include conditions associated with:

```text
wal_removed
idle_timeout
```

This provides stronger evidence than simply observing that a consumer cannot use a slot.

When an invalidation reason is present:

1. preserve the evidence;
2. identify the affected consumer;
3. determine whether normal recovery remains possible;
4. determine whether reconstruction is required;
5. investigate the condition that caused invalidation.

---

## 10. `inactive_since`

PostgreSQL 18 provides:

```text
inactive_since
```

This helps operators determine how long an inactive slot has been inactive.

That is operationally much more useful than:

```text
active = false
```

alone.

For example:

```text
slot inactive for 30 seconds
```

and:

```text
slot inactive for 30 days
```

represent very different investigation priorities.

Even so, elapsed time alone does not prove abandonment. Compare it with:

- maintenance history;
- consumer status;
- ownership records;
- WAL generation rate;
- retained WAL;
- storage capacity; and
- documented recovery expectations.

---

## 11. `wal_keep_size`

`wal_keep_size` controls a minimum amount of historical WAL that PostgreSQL retains in `pg_wal` for standby purposes.

Inspect:

```sql
SHOW wal_keep_size;
```

This can help a temporarily disconnected standby reconnect without requiring archived WAL or reconstruction.

But `wal_keep_size` is not the same mechanism as replication-slot retention.

Do not assume:

```text
wal_keep_size
        =
replication slot retention limit
```

---

## 12. `max_slot_wal_keep_size`

Inspect:

```sql
SHOW max_slot_wal_keep_size;
```

This setting limits how much WAL replication slots are allowed to retain at checkpoint time.

A value of:

```text
-1
```

means replication slots can retain an unlimited amount of WAL through this setting.

That configuration makes storage monitoring particularly important.

A bounded value can reduce unlimited-retention risk, but it introduces another tradeoff:

```text
retention limit reached
        |
        v
required WAL may become unavailable
        |
        v
slot can become unusable
```

Capacity safety and consumer recoverability must therefore be designed together.

---

## 13. `idle_replication_slot_timeout`

PostgreSQL 18 provides:

```text
idle_replication_slot_timeout
```

Inspect it with:

```sql
SHOW idle_replication_slot_timeout;
```

This setting can invalidate replication slots that remain inactive beyond the configured duration.

It can help control abandoned-slot lifecycle risk, but it must be aligned with legitimate outage and maintenance expectations.

For example, a timeout that is shorter than an approved standby maintenance window could invalidate a slot that operators expected to preserve.

Treat timeout configuration as part of replication policy, not merely cleanup automation.

---

## 14. Retention Controls Are Different

The major controls should be understood separately:

```text
wal_keep_size
      |
      +--> minimum WAL retention for standby needs

max_slot_wal_keep_size
      |
      +--> bounds WAL retained because of replication slots

idle_replication_slot_timeout
      |
      +--> controls prolonged slot inactivity lifecycle
```

No single setting replaces monitoring and capacity planning.

---

## 15. Slot Ownership and Governance

Every production replication slot should have an operational identity.

Maintain information such as:

```text
slot_name:
slot_type:
consumer:
environment:
owner/team:
purpose:
expected activity:
expected outage duration:
retention policy:
alerting:
recovery procedure:
decommission procedure:
```

PostgreSQL metadata cannot tell an operator whether an unknown inactive slot is still required by a business service.

An unidentified slot is therefore a governance problem even before it becomes a capacity incident.

---

## 16. Monitoring Requirements

At minimum, monitor:

```text
slot existence
slot activity
inactive duration
restart_lsn
wal_status
safe_wal_size
invalidation_reason
WAL generation rate
pg_wal filesystem usage
filesystem free space
standby/consumer health
replication lag
checkpoint behavior
```

Monitoring should answer two separate questions:

```text
Can the consumer still recover?
```

and:

```text
Can the primary safely continue retaining the required WAL?
```

Both matter.

---

## 17. Capacity Planning

A useful production model considers:

```text
WAL generation rate
        ×
expected consumer outage duration
        =
potential WAL retention requirement
```

But this is only a planning approximation.

Actual capacity design should account for:

- workload peaks;
- batch operations;
- index maintenance;
- bulk ingestion;
- checkpoints;
- archiving behavior;
- multiple slots;
- multiple standbys;
- maintenance windows;
- recovery time;
- alert-response time; and
- filesystem headroom.

Avoid universal rules such as:

```text
10 GB retained WAL = CRITICAL
```

A threshold must be tied to the actual system's capacity and recovery objectives.

---

## 18. Disk-Risk Incident Flow

When `pg_wal` storage pressure occurs:

```text
pg_wal pressure
      |
      v
Identify WAL retention sources
      |
      v
Inspect replication slots
      |
      +--> active?
      +--> inactive_since?
      +--> restart_lsn?
      +--> wal_status?
      +--> safe_wal_size?
      +--> invalidation_reason?
      |
      v
Identify consumer
      |
      v
Check consumer health
      |
      v
Can consumer safely catch up?
      |
      +--> YES --> preserve required WAL if capacity permits
      |
      +--> NO  --> evaluate rebuild/remediation path
      |
      v
Use approved operational procedure
```

Do not begin with slot deletion.

---

## 19. Why Emergency Slot Deletion Is Dangerous

The following command is powerful:

```sql
SELECT pg_drop_replication_slot('slot_name');
```

It removes the slot's WAL-retention requirement.

That can relieve retention pressure, but it can also remove WAL protection required by a standby or another consumer.

Never treat:

```text
disk full
    ->
drop oldest slot
```

as a standard remediation procedure.

Before slot removal, determine:

- what owns the slot;
- whether the consumer is active;
- whether it can reconnect;
- whether it can catch up;
- whether it must be rebuilt;
- whether another recovery source exists;
- whether removal is authorized; and
- how service protection will be restored.

---

## 20. Controlled Slot Removal Procedure

A production slot-removal procedure should include:

1. Identify the slot.
2. Identify its consumer and owner.
3. Confirm whether it is active.
4. Determine why it is inactive if applicable.
5. Capture `restart_lsn`, `wal_status`, `safe_wal_size`, and invalidation evidence.
6. Capture consumer/standby replication state.
7. Determine whether catch-up remains possible.
8. Determine whether the consumer requires reconstruction.
9. Confirm storage urgency.
10. Obtain required change or incident authorization.
11. Preserve evidence.
12. Execute the approved remediation.
13. Validate WAL/storage behavior afterward.
14. Restore or rebuild replication protection.
15. Document the event.

The SAFE-READ lab in this module intentionally stops before destructive remediation.

---

## 21. Standby Safety

A replication slot is one component of standby protection.

Do not assume:

```text
slot exists
    =
standby healthy
```

or:

```text
slot retained WAL
    =
standby can definitely recover
```

Validate the complete replication path:

```text
primary
   |
   v
slot
   |
   v
WAL availability
   |
   v
network
   |
   v
standby receive
   |
   v
standby replay
   |
   v
recoverability
```

Standby safety requires evidence from the complete system.

## 24. Lab Classification Model

The lab uses conservative interpretation:

```text
PASS
INFO
WARNING
CRITICAL
UNKNOWN
```

### Examples

```text
active physical slot
```

can be informational/healthy evidence but does not by itself prove complete standby health.

```text
inactive physical slot
```

requires context and investigation rather than automatic failure.

```text
wal_status = unreserved
```

requires prompt investigation because required WAL is at risk of removal.

```text
wal_status = lost
```

is critical because the slot is no longer usable for its intended WAL continuation.

```text
safe_wal_size = NULL
```

requires configuration and slot-state context and is not automatically failure.

## 26. Key Takeaways

1. Replication slots protect WAL required by replication consumers.
2. The same protection can create primary-side WAL retention pressure.
3. `active = false` does not automatically mean a slot is abandoned.
4. `restart_lsn` is essential evidence for understanding slot WAL requirements.
5. `pg_wal_lsn_diff()` provides WAL-position distance, not exact slot-attributable disk usage.
6. `wal_status` distinguishes important WAL availability conditions.
7. `wal_status = lost` is a critical condition.
8. `safe_wal_size` must be interpreted with retention configuration and slot state.
9. PostgreSQL 18's `inactive_since`, `invalidation_reason`, and `idle_replication_slot_timeout` improve slot lifecycle observability and control.
10. `wal_keep_size` and `max_slot_wal_keep_size` solve different retention problems.
11. Slot management requires ownership, capacity planning, monitoring, and a recovery procedure.
12. Never drop a production replication slot solely because it appears inactive or because `pg_wal` is growing.

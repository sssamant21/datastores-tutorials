# 6.7 — Asynchronous and Synchronous Replication Policy

## Purpose

Streaming replication answers an important question: **where is PostgreSQL WAL being copied?**

A production replication policy must answer a different question:

> **Which remote acknowledgement, if any, must a transaction wait for before PostgreSQL reports COMMIT success?**

This distinction is fundamental. A standby can be healthy and streaming while the primary is still operating asynchronously.

This tutorial builds an operational policy for PostgreSQL 18 physical streaming replication. It focuses on durability, commit availability, latency, failure behavior, observability, and safe production decision-making.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

- explain asynchronous and synchronous physical streaming replication;
- distinguish WAL transport from transaction commit policy;
- explain the roles of `synchronous_standby_names` and `synchronous_commit`;
- distinguish priority-based (`FIRST`) from quorum-based (`ANY`) synchronous replication;
- interpret `pg_stat_replication.sync_state` and `sync_priority`;
- explain how standby loss can affect synchronous commits;
- evaluate replication policy against RPO, latency, topology, and availability requirements;
- collect safe evidence without changing replication configuration; and
- define rollout, monitoring, and rollback requirements for a production policy.

---

## 1. Replication Transport Is Not Commit Policy

A typical physical streaming topology may look like this:

```text
                 WAL stream
Primary  ---------------------------->  Standby A
   |
   +--------------------------------->  Standby B
                 WAL stream
```

This confirms WAL transport.

It does **not** by itself prove that transactions wait for either standby.

A production review therefore separates three layers:

```text
Business durability requirement
            |
            v
Transaction commit policy
            |
            v
WAL transport / standby topology
```

A healthy streaming standby can still have:

```text
sync_state = async
```

That standby is participating in replication, but it is not currently a synchronous standby for commit acknowledgement.

---

## 2. Asynchronous Replication

Physical streaming replication is asynchronous unless synchronous replication is configured for the relevant transaction behavior.

Conceptually:

```text
Application
    |
    v
Primary
    |
    +---- local WAL durability requirement
    |
    +-------------------- WAL --------------------> Standby
    |
    v
COMMIT can complete without waiting for
the standby required by a synchronous policy
```

### Operational characteristics

Asynchronous replication generally provides:

- lower dependency of commit latency on standby/network latency;
- continued commit availability when a standby is unavailable; and
- the possibility that recently committed transactions have not reached the standby at the moment the primary fails.

The exact potential data loss is workload- and failure-dependent. Do not convert an observed lag value into a guaranteed RPO without validating the complete architecture and failure model.

---

## 3. Synchronous Replication

Synchronous replication makes transaction completion depend on acknowledgement from synchronous standby candidates, subject to the configured policy and the transaction's `synchronous_commit` setting.

Conceptually:

```text
Application
    |
    v
Primary ---------------- WAL ----------------> Required standby
    ^                                              |
    |                                              |
    +--------------- acknowledgement -------------+
    |
    v
COMMIT completes
```

This strengthens remote durability guarantees, but creates an operational dependency:

> A required acknowledgement path can become part of application commit latency and commit availability.

Synchronous replication therefore should be treated as a business durability policy, not simply as a "better replication" switch.

---

## 4. Two Controls That Must Not Be Confused

### 4.1 `synchronous_standby_names`

`synchronous_standby_names` determines **which standby servers are candidates and how many acknowledgements are required**.

Inspect it with:

```sql
SHOW synchronous_standby_names;
```

An empty value means no synchronous standby policy is configured through this parameter.

### 4.2 `synchronous_commit`

`synchronous_commit` controls **what level of synchronization the transaction waits for**.

Inspect it with:

```sql
SHOW synchronous_commit;
```

PostgreSQL 18 supports:

```text
remote_apply
on
remote_write
local
off
```

These settings have different acknowledgement semantics.

The critical operational model is:

```text
synchronous_standby_names
        |
        +--> WHO can/must acknowledge

synchronous_commit
        |
        +--> WHAT acknowledgement level is required
```

Do not infer transaction durability from either setting in isolation.

---

## 5. Priority-Based Synchronous Replication

A priority policy can be expressed as:

```conf
synchronous_standby_names = 'FIRST 1 (standby1, standby2)'
```

`FIRST` means priority-based synchronous replication.

With this example:

```text
standby1 -> higher priority
standby2 -> lower priority
```

If both are eligible, the higher-priority standby normally becomes synchronous. Another eligible standby can remain a potential replacement.

A typical observation might resemble:

```text
application_name | sync_state | sync_priority
-----------------+------------+--------------
standby1         | sync       | 1
standby2         | potential  | 2
```

The actual state must always be verified from the running system.

### Production implication

Priority policy is useful when operators intentionally prefer one standby—for example, because of topology or failure-domain design.

The priority order itself becomes production configuration and must therefore be documented.

---

## 6. Quorum-Based Synchronous Replication

A quorum policy can be expressed as:

```conf
synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby1, standby2)'
```

`ANY` means quorum-based synchronous replication.

The example requires an acknowledgement from any one eligible standby.

Conceptually:

```text
              +--> Standby A --+
Primary ------|                |--> any 1 acknowledgement
              +--> Standby B --+
```

With quorum replication, `sync_priority` is not used to select a priority winner.

A standby participating in a quorum policy can report:

```text
sync_state = quorum
```

### Production implication

Quorum configuration can reduce dependency on one specifically preferred standby, but it does not eliminate the need to analyze:

- number of eligible standbys;
- number of required acknowledgements;
- failure domains;
- network paths;
- correlated failures; and
- remaining quorum after maintenance or failure.

## 7. Understanding `pg_stat_replication`

On a primary with connected physical standbys:

```sql
SELECT
    application_name,
    client_addr,
    state,
    sync_state,
    sync_priority,
    write_lag,
    flush_lag,
    replay_lag
FROM pg_stat_replication
ORDER BY application_name;
```

Important `sync_state` values include:

| State | Operational meaning |
|---|---|
| `async` | standby is asynchronous |
| `potential` | asynchronous standby that may become synchronous in a priority configuration |
| `sync` | synchronous standby in a priority-based configuration |
| `quorum` | standby considered a quorum candidate |

Do not interpret:

```text
state = streaming
```

as proof of synchronous protection.

The important distinction is:

```text
streaming = WAL transport state

sync_state = synchronous-policy participation
```

---

## 8. Interpreting Lag Columns Safely

`pg_stat_replication` exposes:

```text
write_lag
flush_lag
replay_lag
```

These values help characterize recent acknowledgement delay.

They must not be treated as universally valid, continuously updated "seconds behind primary" counters.

On a caught-up system with little or no new WAL activity, lag values can eventually become `NULL`.

Therefore:

```text
NULL lag
```

must not automatically be classified as:

```text
FAIL
```

Production monitoring should combine multiple signals, including WAL positions, connection state, replay state, workload activity, and policy requirements.

---

## 9. Failure Behavior

Failure behavior is one of the most important differences between asynchronous and synchronous policy.

### Asynchronous standby unavailable

Conceptually:

```text
Primary --------------------X Standby
   |
   +--> transaction commits continue
```

Replication protection is degraded, but commit completion is not waiting for that standby as a synchronous participant.

### Required synchronous acknowledgement unavailable

Conceptually:

```text
Primary --------------------X Required standby
   |
   v
COMMIT
   |
   v
WAIT
```

The primary database process may still be running and reachable while transactions requiring synchronous acknowledgement wait.

This creates an important production distinction:

```text
Database process availability
            !=
Transaction commit availability
```

An HA design must account for both.

---

## 10. `synchronous_commit` and Per-Transaction Policy

A PostgreSQL deployment can have synchronous standby configuration while allowing individual workloads or transactions to use a different `synchronous_commit` behavior.

For controlled testing:

```sql
BEGIN;

SET LOCAL synchronous_commit = 'on';

-- transaction workload

COMMIT;
```

The setting can also be changed at session scope:

```sql
SET synchronous_commit = 'local';
```

or:

```sql
SET synchronous_commit = 'off';
```

These choices change transaction durability semantics and must not be introduced casually into application sessions.

Production policy should specify whether applications are allowed to override the cluster/default behavior.

---

## 11. Latency Considerations

Synchronous replication adds a remote acknowledgement path to transaction processing.

Commit latency can therefore be influenced by:

- network round-trip time;
- standby storage latency;
- WAL write/flush performance;
- standby resource pressure;
- acknowledgement level;
- number of required acknowledgements; and
- failure or degradation of eligible standbys.

A geographically distant standby can be valuable for disaster recovery while being inappropriate as a synchronous commit dependency for some latency-sensitive workloads.

Topology and commit policy should therefore be designed separately.

## 13. Example Policy Patterns

These are architectural examples, not universal recommendations.

### Pattern A — Asynchronous HA/DR

```text
Primary
   |
   +---- async ----> Standby
```

Useful when commit independence and latency are prioritized and the accepted RPO permits asynchronous replication risk.

### Pattern B — Priority synchronous

```text
                 +--> Standby A [preferred]
Primary ---------|
                 +--> Standby B [candidate]
```

Example:

```conf
synchronous_standby_names = 'FIRST 1 (standby_a, standby_b)'
```

### Pattern C — Quorum synchronous

```text
                 +--> Standby A
Primary ---------|
                 +--> Standby B

Required acknowledgement: ANY 1
```

Example:

```conf
synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby_a, standby_b)'
```

The correct policy depends on requirements and failure analysis.

## 14. Safe Rollout Procedure

Do not introduce synchronous replication directly into a critical environment without validation.

A production rollout should include:

1. Verify replication health.
2. Verify standby `application_name` values.
3. Measure baseline commit latency.
4. Confirm network and storage behavior.
5. Confirm candidate standby failure domains.
6. Define the intended `synchronous_standby_names`.
7. Define the intended `synchronous_commit` behavior.
8. Define what happens if required acknowledgements disappear.
9. Establish monitoring before rollout.
10. Establish rollback criteria.
11. Test in a non-production environment.
12. Use an approved change window for production.
13. Validate application latency and commit behavior after the change.

---

## 15. Rollback Planning

Rollback must be designed before synchronous policy is enabled.

A rollback plan should identify:

- who can authorize the rollback;
- which configuration will be restored;
- how configuration is applied/reloaded;
- how waiting transactions are assessed;
- how replication health is validated afterward; and
- what evidence is retained for incident/change review.

Do not improvise a durability-policy change during an outage.

---

## 16. Monitoring Requirements

At minimum, monitor:

```text
standby connection state
sync_state
replication WAL positions
write/flush/replay lag evidence
standby availability
commit latency
WAL generation
WAL retention
network latency/errors
storage latency
```

Useful primary-side inspection:

```sql
SELECT
    pid,
    application_name,
    client_addr,
    state,
    sent_lsn,
    write_lsn,
    flush_lsn,
    replay_lsn,
    sync_priority,
    sync_state,
    write_lag,
    flush_lag,
    replay_lag
FROM pg_stat_replication
ORDER BY application_name;
```

Monitoring must reflect the actual policy. For example, a synchronous policy requires alerting not merely on "standby disconnected" but on loss of the acknowledgements required by that policy.

## 17. Troubleshooting Flow

Use the following sequence when synchronous replication behavior is unexpected:

```text
1. Am I connected to the expected server?
              |
              v
2. Is this server primary or standby?
              |
              v
3. What is synchronous_standby_names?
              |
              v
4. What is synchronous_commit?
              |
              v
5. Which standbys are connected?
              |
              v
6. What are state and sync_state?
              |
              v
7. Does application_name match the policy?
              |
              v
8. Are required acknowledgements available?
              |
              v
9. What are network/storage/replication conditions?
              |
              v
10. Compare behavior with the documented policy
```

Avoid changing configuration before collecting evidence.

## 19. Lab Result Interpretation

### Healthy asynchronous topology

Possible evidence:

```text
state      = streaming
sync_state = async
```

Interpretation:

```text
PASS: streaming replication exists
INFO: transaction protection is asynchronous for this standby
```

### Priority synchronous topology

Possible evidence:

```text
standby_a = sync
standby_b = potential
```

Interpretation:

```text
PASS: observed states are consistent with priority synchronous replication
```

### Quorum topology

Possible evidence:

```text
standby_a = quorum
standby_b = quorum
```

Interpretation:

```text
PASS: observed states are consistent with quorum participation
```

### No rows from `pg_stat_replication`

Interpretation depends on context.

On a primary:

```text
WARNING: no connected WAL sender replication sessions were observed
```

On a standby:

```text
INFO: pg_stat_replication is a primary-side view;
inspect the upstream connection using standby-side views as appropriate
```

Do not classify an unfamiliar topology as healthy merely because the SQL completed successfully.

## 20. Production Acceptance Checklist

Before marking a synchronous replication policy production-ready:

- [ ] RPO is documented.
- [ ] RTO is documented.
- [ ] commit-latency tolerance is documented.
- [ ] primary and standby failure domains are known.
- [ ] standby `application_name` values are controlled.
- [ ] `synchronous_standby_names` policy is documented.
- [ ] `synchronous_commit` policy is documented.
- [ ] application override policy is documented.
- [ ] failure behavior has been tested safely.
- [ ] monitoring exists for required acknowledgements.
- [ ] maintenance behavior is documented.
- [ ] rollback procedure is tested.
- [ ] operators understand that streaming does not imply synchronous durability.

---

## 21. Key Takeaways

1. Streaming replication and synchronous commit protection are different concepts.
2. `synchronous_standby_names` determines which standbys participate in synchronous policy.
3. `synchronous_commit` determines the transaction's required acknowledgement level.
4. `FIRST` provides priority-based synchronous selection.
5. `ANY` provides quorum-based synchronous selection.
6. A synchronous standby dependency can affect both commit latency and commit availability.
7. `pg_stat_replication.sync_state` is essential evidence when validating the active policy.
8. Lag columns require context and must not be treated as simple permanent "seconds behind" counters.
9. Replication policy must be driven by RPO, topology, latency, and failure requirements.
10. Production changes require monitoring and a tested rollback plan.

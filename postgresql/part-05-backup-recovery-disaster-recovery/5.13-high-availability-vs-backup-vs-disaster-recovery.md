# Part 5.13 — High Availability vs Backup vs Disaster Recovery

**Part:** 5 — Backup, Recovery & Disaster Recovery  
**Target:** PostgreSQL 18  
**Status:** Revised Final / Canonical Edition  
**Acceptance artifact:** `labs/ha-dr-readiness-check.sql`

## 1. Purpose

High availability (HA), backup, and disaster recovery (DR) solve related but different failure problems. A production PostgreSQL design must define all three explicitly rather than assuming that replication, snapshots, or backups provide complete protection.

```text
HA       -> keep/restore service quickly during supported failures
Backup   -> preserve recoverable historical data
DR       -> restore an acceptable service after a major site/platform disaster
```

A standby is not automatically a backup. A backup is not automatically an HA solution. Replication across availability zones is not automatically a complete DR strategy.

## 2. Learning Objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

- distinguish HA, backup, and DR responsibilities;
- map failure scenarios to the appropriate protection layer;
- understand PostgreSQL streaming-replication boundaries;
- distinguish synchronous replication from backup durability;
- explain why logical corruption can replicate to standbys;
- distinguish failover from recovery;
- identify recovery objectives and dependencies;
- evaluate standby, backup, WAL archive, and recovery evidence safely;
- identify single-region and control-plane failure assumptions;
- design recovery validation without changing production state.

## 3. Three Protection Layers

| Layer | Primary purpose | Typical mechanisms |
|---|---|---|
| HA | Reduce service interruption | standby, failover, load balancer/service discovery |
| Backup | Preserve recoverable data/history | `pg_dump`, `pg_basebackup`, snapshots, WAL archive |
| DR | Recover from major failure | remote recovery copy, PITR, alternate infrastructure, runbooks |

A mature architecture commonly uses all three.

## 4. Failure Matrix

| Failure | HA | Backup | DR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary host failure | Usually relevant | Secondary protection | Sometimes |
| AZ failure | Multi-AZ HA may help | Secondary protection | Architecture dependent |
| Accidental `DROP TABLE` | Replication usually propagates it | Critical | May be used |
| Bad application update | May propagate bad writes | Critical | Sometimes |
| Storage corruption | Architecture dependent | Critical | Sometimes |
| Region/site loss | Often insufficient alone | Required recovery source | Critical |
| Ransomware/admin compromise | May affect replicas | Independent/protected backup critical | Critical |
| Long-delayed data error | Usually not solved by HA | Retention critical | Possibly |
| Control-plane failure | Architecture dependent | Recovery evidence required | DR may be required |

## 5. PostgreSQL HA Fundamentals

PostgreSQL supports physical streaming replication between a primary and standby. WAL generated by the primary is transmitted and replayed by the standby.

Conceptually:

```text
Application
    |
    v
Primary PostgreSQL
    |
    +---- WAL stream ----> Standby
```

This can reduce recovery time for supported infrastructure failures.

It does not preserve an independent historical copy of every prior database state.

## 6. Replication Can Replicate Mistakes

If an authorized session executes:

```sql
DROP TABLE critical_table;
```

the resulting WAL changes can be replayed by physical standbys.

Similarly, application-level bad writes can propagate.

Therefore:

```text
replica != independent historical backup
```

Backups and retention provide a separate recovery dimension.

## 7. Synchronous Replication

Synchronous replication can change commit acknowledgement behavior and reduce some data-loss exposure during particular failures.

It does not mean:

```text
RPO = 0 for every disaster
```

and it does not protect against:

- logical deletion;
- malicious changes;
- corruption propagated through the system;
- loss of all participating failure domains;
- unavailable recovery credentials/control plane;
- retention requirements.

Treat synchronous replication as an HA/durability design choice, not a replacement for backup and DR.

## 8. Asynchronous Replication

With asynchronous replication, the primary can acknowledge commits before a standby has received/replayed all corresponding WAL.

During abrupt failover, some recently acknowledged transactions can therefore be absent from the promoted standby.

Measure actual replication lag and design the architecture against the approved recovery objectives.

## 9. Failover Is Not Restore

Failover generally means switching service to another running PostgreSQL instance.

Restore means reconstructing data from recovery artifacts.

```text
Primary failure
   -> standby promotion
   -> failover

Accidental deletion discovered later
   -> select backup/recovery point
   -> restore/PITR
   -> validate
   -> recovery
```

These workflows have different controls, evidence, and risks.

## 10. HA Failure Domains

Document what the architecture actually survives:

```text
process
VM/node
storage device
availability zone
region
cloud account/subscription/project
network/control plane
operator credential compromise
```

Two database instances in one failure domain may provide redundancy without satisfying the required DR objective.

## 11. DR Requires an Alternate Recovery Path

A DR design should answer:

- Where are recovery artifacts stored?
- Are they independent from the primary failure domain?
- Are protected/immutable copies required?
- Can credentials be recovered during an incident?
- Can networking/DNS/service discovery be rebuilt?
- Can encryption keys and secrets be accessed?
- Are PostgreSQL versions/extensions available?
- Is enough compute/storage/network capacity available?
- Who can declare DR?
- How is application acceptance performed?
- How is traffic cut over?
- How is failback handled?

A remote backup alone is not a complete DR plan.

## 12. Recovery Objectives

HA and DR must be tied to approved objectives:

```text
RPO -> acceptable data-loss interval
RTO -> acceptable service-recovery interval
```

Part 5.12 covers capacity planning in detail.

Do not infer business RPO/RTO solely from replication mode, backup frequency, or provider configuration.

## 13. Backup Independence

A strong backup design considers independence from:

- database credentials;
- database host;
- primary storage;
- replication topology;
- region/site;
- administrative deletion;
- compromised credentials.

The required independence level depends on organizational risk and policy.

## 14. WAL Archive Role

Continuous WAL archiving can support PITR and is a recovery mechanism distinct from streaming replication.

A DR design using PITR depends on a complete usable chain:

```text
usable base backup
+
required WAL
+
timeline/history information where applicable
+
restore mechanism
+
recovery infrastructure
```

Archive activity alone does not prove DR readiness.

## 15. Standby Readiness Evidence

Useful PostgreSQL evidence includes:

```sql
SELECT pg_is_in_recovery();
```

On a standby, inspect receiver state through `pg_stat_wal_receiver` where visible.

On a primary, inspect connected replication senders through `pg_stat_replication`.

These views provide runtime evidence, not proof of a complete HA/DR architecture.

## 16. Replication Lag

Useful primary-side evidence can include:

```text
sent_lsn
write_lsn
flush_lsn
replay_lsn
write_lag
flush_lag
replay_lag
sync_state
```

Interpret lag carefully.

A momentary low lag does not prove future failover will meet RPO/RTO.

Lag is workload- and network-dependent.

## 16A. Replication Lag Interpretation

`write_lag`, `flush_lag`, and `replay_lag` are runtime replication evidence, not direct
RPO measurements and not simple predictions of catch-up duration. A primary can also
measure WAL byte distance with `pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), replay_lsn)`,
but byte distance is not time lag and neither measurement proves business data loss.

Configuration and runtime state must be interpreted together. In particular,
`synchronous_standby_names` being configured does not prove the required synchronous
standby is currently available. `synchronous_commit` semantics can also vary by
configuration/session/transaction context; synchronous replication must not be
presented as universal zero-RPO protection.

## 16B. Promotion, Timelines, and Fencing

Promotion is a critical state-changing production action. It can establish a new
authoritative writable server and timeline. Required timeline-history information
must be preserved for recovery workflows.

A production HA design must prevent split brain through architecture-appropriate
fencing/coordination. Removing a server from DNS or a load balancer is not necessarily
equivalent to write fencing because direct clients, jobs, stale pools, or alternate
network paths may still exist.

The acceptance lab never promotes a standby.

## 17. Replication Slots

Slots can help retain WAL required by consumers but can also create disk-pressure risk when consumers stall.

Part 5.10 covers slot lifecycle and WAL retention.

Never automatically drop or advance a slot merely because a readiness check finds it inactive.

## 18. Backup Readiness

HA readiness should be evaluated alongside backup evidence:

- last successful backup;
- artifact verification;
- retention;
- WAL/PITR coverage;
- isolated restore-test evidence;
- last successful application recovery test.

The SQL acceptance lab cannot prove those external facts by itself.

## 19. DR Readiness

DR readiness should include:

```text
recovery artifacts
+ alternate infrastructure
+ IAM/credentials
+ encryption keys
+ network/DNS
+ PostgreSQL dependencies
+ runbook
+ trained operators
+ application validation
+ measured RPO/RTO
```

A document that has never been rehearsed is weaker evidence than a documented, successful recovery exercise.

## 20. Planned vs Unplanned Failover

Planned switchover and emergency failover have different risk profiles.

A planned operation can normally verify synchronization and dependencies before traffic movement.

An emergency failover may require decisions with incomplete information.

Production runbooks should explicitly distinguish the two.

## 21. Split-Brain Risk

Unsafe promotion or routing can allow more than one writable database path.

HA tooling should provide fencing/coordination appropriate to the platform.

This tutorial does not provide automatic promotion commands because promotion is a state-changing production action requiring architecture-specific controls.

## 21A. Asynchronous Failover Data-Loss Assessment

Before an emergency asynchronous failover, capture available evidence such as the last
known primary WAL position, standby received/replayed position, and application
high-watermark. LSN distance alone does not prove exact business data loss. The
decision to accept possible loss belongs to the incident/recovery authority defined
by the organization.

## 22. Failback Is a Separate Procedure

After failover, never assume the former primary remains authoritative or can simply be returned to service. Establish the authoritative source, divergence/timeline relationship, fencing state, and supported rebuild/rejoin method first.

After operating from a DR/standby environment, returning service to the original or rebuilt environment requires its own plan:

- determine authoritative data source;
- rebuild/reseed as required;
- establish replication;
- validate synchronization;
- coordinate traffic;
- preserve recovery evidence;
- obtain change approval.

Do not assume failback is simply "reverse failover."

## 22A. Recovery Access and Correlated Failure Domains

DR planning must include compute, storage, AZ/region, network, DNS, cloud account or
project, IAM, KMS/encryption keys, secrets, backup repository, HA control plane, and
operator credentials. A technically valid encrypted backup is not recoverable if the
authorized recovery team cannot obtain the required key.

Backup independence must consider common administrative and deletion credentials,
not only storage location. Where policy requires it, protected/immutable recovery
copies provide an additional compromise boundary.

## 23. Managed PostgreSQL

Managed services may provide:

- multi-AZ deployment;
- automated failover;
- snapshots/backups;
- PITR;
- cross-region replicas;
- cross-region backup copy.

The exact semantics are provider/service/tier specific.

Provider labels such as "HA" or "multi-AZ" do not by themselves prove the organization's DR requirements are met.

## 24. Kubernetes PostgreSQL

For PostgreSQL on Kubernetes, distinguish:

```text
pod replacement
node replacement
PVC/storage recovery
database failover
backup restore
cluster recreation
regional DR
```

Kubernetes reconciliation is not a substitute for database backup/recovery.

A recreated pod using corrupted or logically damaged persistent data does not solve the data problem.

## 25. Monitoring Domains

Monitor independently:

### HA
- primary/standby role;
- replication connectivity;
- replication lag;
- synchronous state;
- failover tooling/control plane;
- endpoint health.

### Backup
- backup success/failure;
- artifact integrity;
- archive health;
- retention;
- restore-test freshness.

### DR
- remote recovery copy;
- alternate capacity;
- credentials/dependencies;
- rehearsal age;
- observed RPO/RTO;
- unresolved exceptions.

Do not collapse these into a single "database healthy" indicator.

## 28A. Evidence Sensitivity

SAFE-READ does not mean public-safe. Replication addresses, sender hosts, slot names,
database names, and configuration-presence indicators can reveal infrastructure
details. Protect captured evidence according to operational security requirements.
The canonical lab intentionally does not print full `primary_conninfo` or
`archive_command` values.

## 29. Production Safety

### SAFE-READ

Observation of catalogs, statistics, and settings is allowed by this tutorial.

### CHANGE CONTROL REQUIRED

Examples:

- standby promotion;
- switchover/failover;
- synchronous replication changes;
- slot lifecycle changes;
- archive configuration changes;
- backup retention changes;
- DNS/load-balancer routing;
- fencing changes;
- DR activation/failback.

### NEVER AUTOMATE FROM THIS LAB

The acceptance SQL must never interpret an observed issue and automatically execute remediation.

## 30. Acceptance Boundary

A successful lab execution does **not** prove:

```text
HA VALIDATED
BACKUP VALIDATED
DR VALIDATED
RPO ACHIEVED
RTO ACHIEVED
FAILOVER TESTED
RESTORE TESTED
FAILBACK TESTED
```

Those require architecture evidence and controlled exercises.

## 31. Production Readiness Questions

Before calling a PostgreSQL service recovery-ready, ask:

1. What failures does HA cover?
2. What failures require backup restore?
3. What failures trigger DR?
4. Where is the authoritative recovery copy?
5. What is the approved RPO?
6. What is the approved RTO?
7. When was failover last tested?
8. When was restore last tested?
9. When was DR last rehearsed?
10. Was application validation included?
11. Can recovery occur if the primary control plane/account is unavailable?
12. Is failback documented and rehearsed?
13. Are unresolved recovery exceptions owned?

## 31A. Recovery Exercise Closure

A controlled rehearsal should capture evidence, confirm the authoritative topology,
revoke temporary access, sanitize/destroy temporary recovered sensitive data, remove
temporary infrastructure, verify monitoring, record observed RPO/RTO, record
exceptions, assign remediation owners, and close the exercise. Track the freshness of
failover, restore, DR, and failback testing.

## 32. Key Takeaways

1. HA, backup, and DR are complementary, not interchangeable.
2. Replication is not an independent historical backup.
3. Replication can propagate logical mistakes.
4. Failover is not the same as restore.
5. Synchronous replication is not universal zero-RPO protection.
6. DR includes infrastructure, credentials, networking, dependencies, and application validation.
7. PITR requires a complete base-backup/WAL/timeline chain.
8. Failback requires its own controlled procedure.
9. Managed-service HA labels do not prove DR readiness.
10. Recovery readiness is demonstrated through rehearsals and evidence.

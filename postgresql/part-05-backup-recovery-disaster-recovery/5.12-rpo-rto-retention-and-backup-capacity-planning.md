# Part 5.12 — RPO, RTO, Retention, and Backup Capacity Planning

**Part:** 5 — Backup, Recovery & Disaster Recovery  
**Target:** PostgreSQL 18  
**Status:** Revised Final / Canonical Edition  
**Audience:** PostgreSQL DBAs, DBREs, SREs, platform engineers, infrastructure engineers, and service owners

---

## 1. Purpose

A backup design is incomplete until the organization can answer four operational questions:

1. **RPO** — How much committed business data can we tolerate losing?
2. **RTO** — How long can the service remain unavailable?
3. **Retention** — How far back must recovery remain possible?
4. **Capacity** — How much storage, WAL, network throughput, and restore capacity are required to satisfy those objectives?

These are related, but they are not interchangeable.

```text
Business requirements
        |
        +--> RPO
        +--> RTO
        +--> retention
        |
        v
Recovery architecture
        |
        +--> backup frequency
        +--> WAL/archive strategy
        +--> backup generations
        +--> geographic copies
        +--> restore infrastructure
        +--> validation frequency
        |
        v
Capacity + cost + operational readiness
```

PostgreSQL provides backup and recovery mechanisms. It does not decide the business recovery objectives.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

After completing Part 5.12, you should be able to:

- define RPO and RTO without confusing them;
- translate business objectives into backup/recovery requirements;
- distinguish backup retention from WAL/PITR coverage;
- estimate WAL generation from PostgreSQL statistics;
- estimate backup-storage requirements using measured backup sizes;
- calculate approximate WAL archive capacity requirements;
- model daily, weekly, and monthly retention tiers;
- understand why backup compression must be measured rather than guessed;
- include temporary restore capacity in DR planning;
- identify replication-slot and WAL-retention exposure;
- separate HA objectives from backup/DR objectives;
- define recovery-evidence and restore-test requirements;
- build a capacity model without changing production configuration.

## 3. RPO — Recovery Point Objective

RPO describes the maximum acceptable data-loss interval after a disruptive event.

Example:

```text
RPO = 15 minutes
```

means the recovery design should be capable of recovering to a point that satisfies the agreed maximum data-loss interval.

RPO is not the same as backup frequency.

A daily physical backup combined with continuous WAL archiving may support recovery points much newer than the base backup itself, provided the complete required recovery chain remains available and usable.

Conversely:

```text
backup every 15 minutes
```

does not automatically prove:

```text
RPO = 15 minutes
```

The backups must be complete, retained, accessible, restorable, and validated.

---

## 4. RTO — Recovery Time Objective

RTO describes the target maximum elapsed time for restoring an acceptable service after an incident.

Do not measure only database restore runtime.

A production RTO can include:

```text
incident declaration
      +
recovery decision
      +
backup selection
      +
recovery infrastructure provisioning
      +
backup retrieval
      +
database restore
      +
WAL replay / PITR
      +
database validation
      +
application validation
      +
traffic cutover
      =
observed recovery time
```

A five-minute PostgreSQL startup does not demonstrate a 5-minute RTO if infrastructure provisioning and application validation require two hours.

---

## 5. RPO and RTO Are Independent Dimensions

Example:

| Requirement | Objective |
|---|---:|
| RPO | 15 minutes |
| RTO | 2 hours |
| Operational retention | 35 days |
| Monthly archive retention | 12 months |

The architecture might use:

- periodic physical base backups;
- continuous WAL archiving;
- immutable backup storage;
- geographically separated copies;
- recurring restore tests;
- prebuilt recovery automation.

Another workload could have:

```text
RPO = 24 hours
RTO = 8 hours
```

and require a much simpler architecture.

Do not copy recovery objectives from another service simply because both use PostgreSQL.

---

## 6. Retention Is Not RPO

Retention answers:

> How long must a recoverable historical state remain available?

RPO answers:

> How much recent data loss is acceptable?

These solve different problems.

A system may require:

```text
RPO:       15 minutes
Retention: 35 days
```

Continuous WAL/PITR coverage can address recovery-point granularity while the retention policy determines how long backup generations and required recovery artifacts remain available.

---

## 7. Retention Tiers

A common policy model uses multiple generations:

```text
daily backups   -> retain N days
weekly backups  -> retain N weeks
monthly backups -> retain N months
yearly backups  -> retain N years
```

This is only a policy framework.

The actual retention design must account for:

- legal/regulatory requirements;
- security requirements;
- ransomware recovery;
- operational rollback needs;
- audit requirements;
- data classification;
- storage cost;
- restore time;
- backup dependencies;
- PITR/WAL dependencies.

Do not delete recovery artifacts solely because a calendar threshold was reached unless the complete retention and dependency policy permits deletion.

---

## 8. Backup Chain Dependency

Capacity planning must understand the recovery chain.

For a continuous-archive design:

```text
usable base backup
        +
required WAL history
        +
timeline history where applicable
        +
restore configuration
        =
candidate PITR recovery chain
```

Deleting a base backup may make older archived WAL operationally useless for the intended recovery workflow.

Deleting required WAL can make an otherwise intact base backup incapable of reaching a requested recovery point.

Retention therefore must be dependency-aware.

---


## 8A. Effective PITR Coverage

A configured backup-retention period is not automatically the effective PITR
window.

```text
effective PITR coverage
    <= intersection(
         usable retained base-backup coverage,
         required WAL availability,
         required timeline/history availability
       )
```

Retention and deletion automation must preserve the complete dependency chain.
Backup or archived-WAL deletion is a destructive recovery operation and must
follow dependency-aware retention policy and change controls.

## 9. Measure the Database Before Planning Backup Capacity

Useful database-level size evidence:

```sql
SELECT
    datname,
    pg_database_size(datname) AS size_bytes,
    pg_size_pretty(pg_database_size(datname)) AS size_pretty
FROM pg_catalog.pg_database
WHERE datallowconn
ORDER BY pg_database_size(datname) DESC;
```

Cluster database totals are useful planning evidence, but they are not equal to:

- physical backup size;
- compressed backup size;
- WAL archive size;
- object-storage consumption;
- total DR footprint.

Measure actual backup artifacts separately.

---

## 10. Measure WAL Generation

PostgreSQL exposes cumulative WAL statistics through `pg_stat_wal`.

Useful evidence includes:

```sql
SELECT
    wal_records,
    wal_fpi,
    wal_bytes,
    stats_reset
FROM pg_catalog.pg_stat_wal;
```

`wal_bytes` is cumulative since the statistics reset.

Therefore this is incorrect:

```text
wal_bytes = daily WAL generation
```

To measure WAL rate, collect two observations:

```text
T1: wal_bytes_1
T2: wal_bytes_2

delta_wal_bytes = wal_bytes_2 - wal_bytes_1
elapsed_seconds = T2 - T1

wal_bytes_per_second =
    delta_wal_bytes / elapsed_seconds
```

Then derive:

```text
estimated_daily_wal =
    wal_bytes_per_second * 86400
```

Use representative windows. WAL generation can vary dramatically during batch processing, bulk loads, index maintenance, ETL, VACUUM-related activity, and application peaks.

---


## 10A. WAL-Rate Sampling Integrity

A WAL-rate calculation is valid only when the cumulative-statistics baseline is
continuous across both samples.

```text
capture T1: wal_bytes, stats_reset
capture T2: wal_bytes, stats_reset

stats_reset(T1) == stats_reset(T2)?
    NO  -> rate UNKNOWN; discard the interval
    YES -> calculate the delta
```

Also investigate rather than calculate a negative rate if
`wal_bytes(T2) < wal_bytes(T1)`. Never convert missing, inaccessible, reset, or
otherwise incomparable evidence into zero.

Apply the same baseline-aware interpretation to cumulative archiver counters.

## 11. PostgreSQL 18 Statistics Boundary

PostgreSQL 18 changed some WAL I/O statistics compared with older releases.

Do not build a PostgreSQL 18 acceptance lab around removed legacy `pg_stat_wal` write/sync columns.

For Part 5.12, `pg_stat_wal.wal_bytes` is used only as cumulative WAL-generation evidence.

I/O latency/counters belong to the appropriate PostgreSQL 18 I/O monitoring views and infrastructure telemetry.

---

## 12. WAL Capacity Model

A simple planning model is:

```text
WAL archive capacity
    ~= measured WAL/day
       * WAL retention days
       * safety factor
```

Example only:

```text
Measured WAL/day = 180 GB
PITR retention   = 14 days
Raw WAL          = 2.52 TB
```

Do not apply an arbitrary compression percentage as a production fact.

Measure the actual archive technology, compression configuration, workload, and object-storage behavior.

---

## 13. Peak WAL Matters More Than Average WAL During Incidents

Suppose:

```text
average WAL rate = 2 MB/s
peak WAL rate    = 20 MB/s
```

A capacity model based only on the average can substantially underestimate:

- archive bandwidth;
- temporary `pg_wal` growth;
- replication-slot retention;
- recovery transfer rate;
- storage runway during archive failure.

Track both normal and peak/burst behavior.

---

## 14. Archive Health Is Part of RPO Risk

Inspect:

```sql
SELECT
    archived_count,
    failed_count,
    last_archived_wal,
    last_archived_time,
    last_failed_wal,
    last_failed_time,
    stats_reset
FROM pg_catalog.pg_stat_archiver;
```

This provides archiver activity evidence.

It does **not** prove:

- every required WAL object exists in remote storage;
- archived objects are readable;
- archive retention is correct;
- a complete PITR chain exists;
- the restore command can retrieve the files.

Those claims require backup-system and restore testing evidence.

---

## 15. Replication Slots Affect Capacity

Inspect:

```sql
SELECT
    slot_name,
    slot_type,
    active,
    restart_lsn,
    wal_status,
    safe_wal_size,
    inactive_since,
    invalidation_reason
FROM pg_catalog.pg_replication_slots
ORDER BY slot_name;
```

A stalled slot can retain significant WAL locally.

Therefore the capacity model should include:

```text
normal WAL footprint
+
checkpoint/recycling behavior
+
archive backlog exposure
+
replication-slot exposure
+
operational safety margin
```

Part 5.10 contains the detailed slot-risk model.

---

## 16. Physical Backup Capacity

Do not estimate physical backup storage solely from database logical sizes.

Measure real artifacts.

A practical model:

```text
backup_storage =
    sum(retained measured backup artifact sizes)
    +
    retained WAL/archive objects
    +
    manifests/metadata
    +
    temporary backup workspace
    +
    restore-test workspace
    +
    safety margin
```

If incremental backup technology is used, model the actual dependency chain and measured incremental sizes rather than treating every generation as a full backup.

---

## 17. Logical Backup Capacity

For logical backups, measure actual output from:

- `pg_dump`;
- `pg_dumpall`;
- directory-format archives;
- custom-format archives;
- compression settings actually used.

Logical database size is not a reliable substitute for archive size.

Also include cluster-global backup artifacts where the recovery design requires roles, tablespaces, or other global objects.

---

## 18. Restore Capacity Is Often Forgotten

Backup capacity and restore capacity are different.

Recovery may require temporary space for:

```text
downloaded backup
+
decompressed/unpacked backup
+
restored PostgreSQL data
+
WAL staging
+
temporary sort/index build space
+
logs
+
validation outputs
```

A recovery design that stores backups successfully but cannot provision enough space to restore them does not meet its objective.

---


## 18A. Recovery Capacity Reserve

Recovery infrastructure should not be sized exactly to the latest observed
requirement. Include an explicit recovery-capacity reserve based on growth
uncertainty, peak WAL bursts, restore concurrency, storage/API throttling,
retention overlap, failed-job retries, and operational response time.

The margin is an organizational design decision, not a PostgreSQL constant.

## 18B. Unknown Is Not Zero

Treat permission failures, unavailable provider metrics, NULL/missing
telemetry, and collection failures as **UNKNOWN / EVIDENCE UNAVAILABLE**.
Never silently convert unavailable evidence into zero usage, zero WAL, or zero
failures.

SAFE-READ also does not mean zero operational cost. Size functions can perform
meaningful filesystem work on large installations; schedule collection at an
appropriate frequency and observe runtime.

## 19. Network Capacity and RTO

Large backups can make network throughput a dominant RTO component.

Approximate transfer time:

```text
transfer_seconds =
    backup_bytes / effective_bytes_per_second
```

Use effective measured throughput, not interface line rate.

Account for:

- encryption;
- object-store API behavior;
- cross-region transfer;
- throttling;
- concurrent recovery traffic;
- compression/decompression;
- storage read/write throughput.

---

## 20. Restore Throughput

Measure restore throughput during rehearsals.

For example:

```text
effective_restore_rate =
    restored_bytes / restore_elapsed_seconds
```

But do not turn this directly into the entire RTO.

Restore elapsed time is only one stage of end-to-end recovery.

---

## 21. Capacity Forecasting

A simple data-growth model:

```text
current_data_size
+
(expected_daily_growth * planning_days)
=
projected_data_size
```

Then re-evaluate:

- backup duration;
- backup storage;
- WAL generation;
- network transfer;
- restore duration;
- recovery infrastructure;
- validation time.

Linear growth is only a planning approximation. Use historical growth data and workload forecasts whenever possible.

---

## 22. Backup Window

A backup must finish with enough operational margin.

Track:

```text
backup start
backup end
backup duration
artifact size
average throughput
peak workload impact
success/failure
verification status
```

Trend backup duration over time.

A backup that once completed in two hours can eventually exceed its maintenance or recovery design window as the database grows.

---

## 23. Retention Capacity Worksheet

A planning worksheet might contain:

| Component | Inputs |
|---|---|
| Full/base backups | measured size × retained generations |
| Incremental backups | measured generation sizes |
| Logical backups | measured archive sizes |
| WAL archive | measured WAL rate × retention |
| Safety margin | policy-defined |
| Restore workspace | measured/forecast requirement |
| Cross-region copy | retained replica footprint |
| Immutable copy | retained immutable footprint |
| Metadata/manifests | measured footprint |
| Growth | forecast horizon |

Avoid double counting when the backup platform uses deduplication or shared storage.

Also avoid assuming deduplication savings without measured platform evidence.

---


## 23A. Dependency-Aware Cleanup and Protected Copies

Never delete the only validated recovery set merely to relieve capacity
pressure. A safe response hierarchy is:

1. measure remaining capacity and runway;
2. identify the growth source;
3. identify lifecycle failures and unexpectedly retained generations;
4. validate backup/WAL/timeline dependencies;
5. add emergency capacity when supported and approved;
6. correct failed lifecycle or archive processes;
7. delete only artifacts proven eligible under the retention policy.

Capacity models must separately include protected/immutable copies,
cross-region copies, WAL/archive objects, and temporary restore workspace.

When PostgreSQL incremental backup workflows are used, model the measured
retained dependency chain rather than multiplying backup count by full-backup
size. Compression and deduplication savings must likewise be measured rather
than assumed.

## 24. RPO Evidence

A recovery test should prove RPO using an agreed business or workload marker.

Examples:

- known transaction ID;
- application event timestamp;
- ingestion high-watermark;
- batch ID;
- business sequence number.

Do not declare RPO success solely because the database reached a particular WAL LSN.

The business requirement concerns recoverable application state.

---

## 25. RTO Evidence

Capture timestamps for major recovery milestones:

```text
T0 incident/recovery exercise begins
T1 recovery decision
T2 target infrastructure ready
T3 backup retrieval begins
T4 database restore begins
T5 WAL replay/recovery completes
T6 database validation completes
T7 application validation completes
T8 service accepted
```

Then calculate the RTO according to the organization's agreed start and end definitions.

Document those definitions before the incident.

---

## 26. Recovery Objectives Need Ownership

Maintain an inventory such as:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Service | Recovery scope |
| Business owner | Accepts objective |
| Technical owner | Implements recovery |
| RPO | Data-loss objective |
| RTO | Service-recovery objective |
| Retention | Historical recovery requirement |
| Backup method | Logical/physical/provider |
| PITR | Required/not required |
| Restore-test frequency | Validation cadence |
| Last successful test | Evidence freshness |
| Observed RPO | Test result |
| Observed RTO | Test result |
| Exceptions | Accepted gaps |

Database administrators should not invent business RPO/RTO values.

---

## 27. HA Is Not a Substitute for Backup

High availability can reduce outage duration for some infrastructure failures.

It does not automatically protect against:

- accidental deletion;
- application corruption;
- malicious changes;
- delayed discovery of bad data;
- logical mistakes replicated to standbys;
- retention requirements.

Likewise, backups are not a substitute for an HA architecture when very short service interruption objectives are required.

Part 5.13 covers this distinction in detail.

---

## 28. Managed-Service Considerations

Managed PostgreSQL platforms can abstract:

- physical backup storage;
- snapshot creation;
- WAL retention;
- PITR implementation;
- cross-region copies;
- restore provisioning.

Still validate:

```text
configured retention
+
actual available recovery window
+
restore permissions
+
restore target capacity
+
observed restore duration
+
application validation
```

Do not treat a control-plane setting as proof of achieved RPO or RTO.

---

## 29. Capacity Alerting

Useful alert domains include:

- backup job failures;
- backup duration growth;
- backup artifact-size growth;
- archive failures;
- archive freshness;
- `pg_wal` filesystem utilization;
- replication-slot retention;
- backup repository capacity;
- object-storage lifecycle failures;
- restore-test failures;
- recovery-evidence age.

Alerts should map to an owner and an operational response.

---

## 30. Safety Margin

Capacity plans need margin because recovery workloads occur under abnormal conditions.

Do not size backup/restore infrastructure to exactly the most recent observed requirement.

Safety margin should be based on:

- growth uncertainty;
- peak WAL bursts;
- restore concurrency;
- infrastructure provisioning delay;
- storage/API throttling;
- retention overlap;
- failed-job retries;
- operational response time.

The percentage or amount is an organizational design decision, not a PostgreSQL constant.

## 33. Production Planning Workflow

```text
Step 1  Obtain approved RPO/RTO/retention requirements
Step 2  Inventory backup/recovery architecture
Step 3  Measure database and backup sizes
Step 4  Measure WAL rate across representative windows
Step 5  Measure backup duration and throughput
Step 6  Measure restore duration and throughput
Step 7  Model retained backup + WAL capacity
Step 8  Add growth forecast and safety margin
Step 9  Validate restore infrastructure capacity
Step 10 Run isolated recovery rehearsal
Step 11 Measure achieved RPO/RTO
Step 12 Record gaps and remediation owners
Step 13 Repeat on an approved cadence
```

## 34. Production Safety Rules

### SAFE-READ

The acceptance lab may:

- read catalog sizes;
- read cumulative statistics;
- inspect configuration;
- inspect replication-slot state;
- inspect tablespace metadata;
- inspect recovery state.

### APPROVAL REQUIRED

Production changes such as these require the normal change-management process:

- retention-policy changes;
- archive destination/lifecycle changes;
- `wal_keep_size` changes;
- `max_slot_wal_keep_size` changes;
- backup schedule changes;
- replication-slot lifecycle changes;
- recovery architecture changes.

### NEVER AUTOMATE FROM THIS LAB

The acceptance artifact must not:

```text
delete backup generations
delete WAL archive objects
drop/advance replication slots
force CHECKPOINT
force WAL switch
reset statistics
change PostgreSQL configuration
change object-storage lifecycle
```

## 35. Acceptance Boundary

This lab provides planning evidence.

It does not prove:

- business RPO is satisfied;
- business RTO is satisfied;
- backup retention is correct;
- backup artifacts are valid;
- remote WAL exists;
- PITR succeeds;
- backup repository capacity is sufficient;
- a restore can complete in the required time.

Those require backup-platform evidence, infrastructure metrics, policy records, and recurring restore rehearsals.

---

## 36. Key Takeaways

1. RPO, RTO, retention, and capacity are separate design dimensions.
2. Backup frequency alone does not prove RPO.
3. Restore runtime alone does not prove RTO.
4. Retention must account for recovery-chain dependencies.
5. Measure actual backup sizes and WAL generation.
6. `pg_stat_wal.wal_bytes` is cumulative evidence, not a daily rate.
7. Plan for peaks, not only averages.
8. Include restore workspace and network capacity.
9. Include replication-slot and archive-failure exposure.
10. Measure achieved RPO/RTO through recovery rehearsals.
11. Managed-service configuration does not replace recovery validation.
12. Business owners define acceptable recovery objectives; engineering demonstrates whether the architecture meets them.
